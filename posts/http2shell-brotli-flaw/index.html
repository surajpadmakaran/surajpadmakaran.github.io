The Flaw Was Hiding in the Compression Library

A tiny program that unpacks compressed web data — built into websites, apps and tools you use every day — had a mistake in it. In October it went from discovery to confirmed attacks in six days. Here is what happened, in plain English.

Oct 30, 20265 min readExplainedBy Suraj Padmakaran

Almost everything you load on the web travels shrink-wrapped. Text, images and code are compressed before they cross the internet so pages arrive faster, then unpacked by your browser or by the website's servers. The most common shrink-wrap is a Google technology called Brotli. You have never seen it, never installed it, and used it hundreds of times today.

That invisible helper is where the problem was hiding.

A pocket too small for what it was handed

Inside Brotli sits a small “unpacker” program. In October, researchers found that if it is handed a specially malformed package, it makes a quiet error: instead of rejecting the package, it keeps stuffing incoming data into a pocket that holds only eight bytes — roughly eight characters of text. The data spills over the sides and overwrites whatever sits next to it in the computer's memory.

Think of a receptionist who keeps forcing parcels into a full locker. Eventually the parcels crush the things stored in the neighbouring lockers. In a computer, “the things next door” can be other data — or instructions the machine is about to follow. That is how one malformed package can crash a program, leak information it shouldn't, or, in the worst case, hand an attacker control of it.

The flaw has an official number — CVE-2026-111497 — and a string of nicknames as it spread: Node2Shell, Python2Shell, HTTP2Shell. Experts rate it 9.2 out of 10 for severity.

Why one small bug reached so far

The unpacker is not a product anyone buys. It is a building block, copied into other software for free — and it had been copied into a lot of software.

It sits inside Node.js, the engine behind a large share of the world's websites and apps, and inside popular Python tools used by millions of programs. From there the list kept growing: Microsoft's .NET platform, data tools like DuckDB and ClickHouse, archive programs, even software that opens fonts and documents. Many of those copies never touch the internet at all — a booby-trapped document or font file can reach the same unpacker. And because each copy is built into its host program, fixing the original doesn't fix the copies. Every product has to repair its own.

It can also be triggered in two directions. A stranger can send a trapped package to a vulnerable website — or a program can be tricked into fetching one from a hostile server, the digital equivalent of picking up a parcel that explodes on your own doorstep.

Six days from discovery to confirmed attacks

The timeline is the part worth remembering. Fixes for the mainstream tools were published on October 21 and 22 — genuinely fast. Working demonstration attacks appeared within about a day. On October 26, a security firm recorded a real attack attempt in the wild: a 23-megabyte trapped package aimed at a website's unpacker. On October 27, the US cybersecurity agency CISA placed the flaw on its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities list — the government's catalogue of flaws attackers are confirmed to be using — and gave federal agencies until November 10 to fix it.

One honest caveat: experts still argue about how reliably the worst case — full takeover — can be pulled off. It has been demonstrated in laboratories; some specialists rate it harder than the headlines suggest. What nobody disputes is the crash: any exposed service can be knocked over with a single trapped package. When a flaw is confirmed in use and the fix already exists, that argument doesn't change what you should do.

The patch existed within a day. The hard part was knowing where all the copies were.

What this means if you don't work in security

If you run a website or an app, the practical questions are simple. Are you on a supported version of your platform? One popular engine, Node.js 20, is past its end of life and will receive no fix at all — anything still running on it has to be upgraded or taken out of harm's way. Does your hosting provider patch the platform for you, and have they? And do you know which of your tools quietly contain this unpacker? “We updated the main system” is not the same as “we fixed every copy.”

If you don't run anything, there's nothing you need to do — your browser maker handles its own copy. But it's worth understanding the shape of the problem, because it repeats: the modern world runs on shared building blocks, most of them maintained for free, all of them copied everywhere.

The takeaway

Every few years, a flaw arrives that is really a counting problem in disguise. The patch existed within a day. The hard part was never the repair — it was knowing where all the copies were. You cannot fix what you don't know you have, and this October, a remarkable number of organizations found out they were running a tiny unpacking program they had never heard of, in places they never thought to look.

Patch the big systems now. Then go looking for the copies nobody knew about.
