Ransomware Still Walks In Through the Front Door

A ransomware gang breached more than 600 organizations in two years — not by breaking in, but by logging in. The same front door exists in every home. Here is how it works, and how to lock yours.

Oct 28, 20265 min readExplainedBy Suraj Padmakaran

Ransomware is extortion software. Criminals get into a computer or a company's network, lock up its files so nothing can be opened, and demand payment to hand back the key. Usually they steal a copy first, so they can threaten to publish your data if you refuse to pay.

On October 20, the US cybersecurity agency CISA, the FBI, the Secret Service and partners in Germany and the UK published a joint warning about one such gang, called SafePay. Active since August 2024, it has hit at least 600 organizations across the Americas, Europe and Australia — including hospitals and other services communities depend on. The interesting part of the warning is not the gang. It is the door they used.

They didn't break in. They logged in.

The warning lists exactly how SafePay gets inside: stolen or guessed passwords, trick emails that harvest logins, password-guessing attacks, abuse of the remote-access tunnels employees use to work from home, and a handful of old, already-fixed software flaws — the oldest from 2020 — that the victims simply never got around to repairing.

Read that list again. There is no master-hacker magic in it. The gang knocks on the front door with somebody else's key, and in more than 600 cases, the door opened.

And once inside, their first move is telling: they hunt down the backups and destroy them. Deleting your way back is not an afterthought in the plan. It is a step in it, done early, so that when the lock clicks shut there is no spare key.

The same door exists in your house

Swap the company network for a family and every item on that list has a home version. The email password that is also the bank password. The login code read out over the phone to a convincing voice claiming to be 'support.' The update postponed for the fortieth time. The box of family photos kept on one computer and nowhere else — with the backup drive plugged into that same computer, where it can be locked up too.

The stakes are not abstract. In its October 2026 report to Congress, the FTC counted roughly $3 billion in fraud losses reported by Americans aged 60 and over in 2025 alone — five times the figure from 2020 — and most of those losses began the same way SafePay begins: a password, a code, or a person persuaded to open the door.

Five locks for a family front door

  • Give every account its own password. If one is stolen, the thief gets one room, not the whole house. Nobody can remember a hundred passwords — that is what a password manager is for. It remembers; you remember one.
  • Turn on two-step verification, starting with email and banking. After your password, the account asks for a code from your phone. A stolen password on its own stops working. Think of it as a second lock that needs something in your pocket, not just something in your head.
  • Install updates when they are offered. Updates are, very often, someone quietly repairing a door that criminals have already found. Postponing them leaves the door as it was.
  • Keep one copy of what you cannot replace somewhere separate. Family photos, important documents: one copy that is not always connected to your main computer — a drive you plug in, copy to, and unplug, or a reputable cloud backup. A copy that can be reached by whatever locks your computer is not a spare key. It is a second target.
  • Make it a family rule: passwords and codes are never given out on a call or in a text. Not to the bank, not to 'Microsoft,' not to anyone. If a call like that comes, hang up and dial the number printed on your card. Real institutions survive being checked. Scammers cannot.

The takeaway

Six hundred organizations — most with IT departments, budgets and security tools — left the front door open for the most ordinary reasons. The lesson for the rest of us is almost comforting: the protection that works is not exotic, expensive or technical. It is five habits, an afternoon to set up, and a family that has agreed on them in advance.

Criminals are voting with their effort. They knock on front doors because front doors open. Lock yours.
