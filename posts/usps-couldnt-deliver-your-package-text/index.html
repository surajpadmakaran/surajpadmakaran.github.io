“USPS Couldn’t Deliver Your Package” Text? The Redelivery Fee Is a Scam

It looks like a helpful note about a parcel. It is actually a thief holding out a card machine — and the defence takes ten seconds and costs nothing.

Nov 4, 20268 min readText-message scamsBy Suraj Padmakaran

Margaret is standing in her kitchen when her phone buzzes. She is, in fact, waiting for a parcel — a birthday present for her granddaughter, ordered two days ago — so the message feels like it was written for her:

"USPS: We could not deliver your package. To reschedule, confirm your details and pay the redelivery fee here:" — followed by a link.

The fee is small. The page behind the link looks exactly like the post office's own website — the same colours, the same logo, a box for her name and address, and a box for her card. She fills it in. The parcel, of course, never comes. What comes, a few days later, is a call from her bank about charges she has never seen, in shops she has never visited.

Margaret did everything the message asked. That was the problem — the message was never from the post office at all.

This trick has a name: smishing — a scam that arrives by text message. The word is simply "SMS" (the technical name for a text) joined to "phishing" (pretending to be someone trustworthy in order to steal from you). And of all the scams that arrive by text, this is the biggest. The Federal Trade Commission (FTC), the US agency that tracks fraud reports from the public, found that fake package-delivery problems were the most reported text scam of 2024 — usually pretending to be the U.S. Postal Service. Altogether, Americans reported $470 million lost to scams that began with a text message in 2024 — more than five times the figure for 2020 (FTC).

How it works

The machinery is simple, and it runs on volume.

The text goes out to millions of phones at once. The criminals do not know you are expecting a parcel. They do not need to. In any given week, a great many of us are expecting one — so if the text lands on enough phones, it lands on the right phones. If it reached you on the one day you were waiting for a delivery, that feels like knowledge. It is only arithmetic.

The link leads to a stage set. The page it opens copies a real carrier — the post office, UPS, FedEx — down to the logo and the wording. It asks for your name and address (which makes it feel official) and then, for a small "redelivery fee" or "customs charge," your card number, its expiry date, and the three digits on the back.

The fee is not the prize. Your card is. A charge of a dollar or two is nothing to the people behind these pages. What they want is the card itself — the complete set of numbers that lets them spend as if they were you. The small fee is only there to make you type the numbers in. Charges you never made tend to follow within days.

Why it works

Three quiet facts do the criminals' work for them, and none of them is your fault.

Texts get read. The FTC notes that text messages are opened at rates estimated as high as 98% — compared with about 20% for email. A scam that arrives by text is a scam that will almost certainly be seen, often within minutes, often while you are busy doing something else.

Everybody is expecting something. Online shopping has made "a parcel is on its way" the most ordinary sentence in modern life. The criminals are not guessing about your life; they are borrowing a sentence that is true for nearly everyone, nearly all the time.

The amount feels too small to be a trick. We are all braced for the big theft — the emptied account, the huge demand. A fee smaller than the price of a coffee slips under that guard. It feels like administration, not danger. That is precisely why it is set so low.

And underneath all three sits a small, familiar pressure: your parcel is waiting, and something is wrong with it. A problem, a deadline, a thing to fix quickly. Hurry is the oldest tool in the scammer's kit, and it works just as well in a text as it does in a phone call.

It travels in a family

The delivery text is the most reported of its kind, but it is worth meeting its relatives, because the defence against all of them is the same. In the FTC's account of 2024, five text scams together made up about half of all reported text fraud: the fake delivery problem; phony job offers; fake fraud alerts pretending to be your bank; unpaid-toll notices; and the friendly "wrong number" text that is not wrong at all, but the opening line of a long conversation designed to empty your savings. Different costumes, one skeleton: a message you did not ask for, a link or a conversation, and a reason to act before you think. Once you have seen the skeleton, the costumes stop working.

The rules that stop it

You do not need new software, a new phone, or a suspicious nature. You need three habits.

1. Never tap the link in a delivery text. Not to check, not to look, not "just to see." The link is the trap; there is no safe way to peek at it. This one habit, on its own, defeats the entire scam.

2. Real carriers do not collect money through text-message links. A text asking you to pay a fee to release, reschedule, or clear a parcel is not how the post office or the big couriers do business. Treat any delivery text that wants payment as fake until you have proven otherwise — by the method in the next rule, never by replying to the text.

3. Go to the carrier yourself, by a road you trust. If a text has genuinely worried you, close it, and check in your own way: open the carrier's own app if you have it, or type the address you know into your browser, or find the tracking number on the receipt or order email from the shop you bought from. If there is a real problem with a real parcel, it will be waiting for you there. If there is nothing there, there was never a problem — only a text.

A useful extra check: think about whether you are expecting anything at all, and from whom. A "delivery problem" for a parcel you never ordered is a lie by definition. And even when you are expecting something, rule 3 still applies — check the real way, not the text's way.

If you already paid

First, the most important sentence in this section: you are in very large company, and the company includes careful, intelligent people. A scam reported $470 million times over in a single year (FTC) is not a trap for the foolish; it is an industry aimed at everyone. Embarrassment is part of its design — it is what makes people stay quiet. Do not give it that help. Then, in this order:

1. Call your bank or card company now — the number on the back of the card, or on your statement; never a number from the text. Tell them you entered your card details on a page you now believe was fake.

2. Dispute any charges you do not recognise, and ask the bank to cancel the card and issue a new one. The numbers are out of your hands now; the card itself has to go.

3. Watch your statements closely for the next few months. Stolen card details are sometimes used quickly and sometimes held and used later, in small amounts designed not to be noticed.

4. Report it to the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov. Your report takes a few minutes, and it is how the FTC builds the picture that warns everyone else — the figures quoted in this chapter exist because people reported. The FTC itself notes that the vast majority of fraud is never reported at all, which means the true cost of these texts is far higher than $470 million. Every report shrinks that blind spot a little.

If this happened

Do these now, in this order. Small steps, nothing technical.

1. Stop. Don’t tap anything else in the text, and don’t reply to it. If the page is still open on your phone, close it.

2. If you entered your card details, call your bank now. Use the number on the back of your card or on your statement — never a number from the text. Say you entered your card details on a page you now believe was fake. Ask the bank to cancel the card, issue a new one, and dispute any charges you don’t recognise.

3. Watch your statements closely for the next few months. Stolen card details are sometimes used later, in small amounts designed not to be noticed.

4. Report it. The FTC takes reports at ReportFraud.ftc.gov — a few minutes, and your report is how the next warning gets written. If money left your account another way — a transfer, a payment app — report it to the FBI as well, at ic3.gov.

5. Tell one person. Forward this chapter to one person who orders online. The same text is on its way to their phone too.

A real parcel can wait. Only a fake one is in a hurry.
