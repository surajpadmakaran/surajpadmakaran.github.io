Two Locks on the Front Door

A password can be copied, leaked, or tricked out of you — and these days, most are. A second lock means that even a thief holding the right password is still standing outside.

Nov 14, 202610 min readTwo-step verificationBy Suraj Padmakaran

The text arrived at twenty past six in the morning, while Margaret was making tea.

It was from her email provider, and it held a six-number code — the kind you type in to prove a sign-in is really yours. The puzzle was simple: Margaret had not signed in. She was standing in her kitchen in her dressing gown, and her laptop was closed.

Somewhere, someone else had her password. They had typed it in correctly — the system accepted it — and then the sign-in had asked for the code. The code had come to her phone, because that is where codes go. The stranger did not have her phone. So the stranger did the only thing left to do: gave up and went away.

Margaret finished her tea, changed her password to a fresh one she had never used before, and got on with her day. Total damage: one confusing text message.

That is the whole subject of this chapter, and it is one of the most comforting facts in this book: a stolen password, on its own, does not have to mean a stolen account. It depends entirely on whether you have fitted the second lock.

Why one lock is no longer enough

The last chapter was about passwords — making them long, making every account different, and letting a password manager do the remembering. All of that still matters. But honesty requires a hard truth alongside it: passwords escape.

They escape when a company's customer records are stolen — a data breach, private information taken from a system without permission. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3), which collects America's internet-crime reports, counted 67,456 personal-data-breach complaints in 2025, with about $1.31 billion in reported losses. Passwords also escape when we are tricked into typing them into a convincing fake page, and — as the last chapter explained — when a password used in one place is simply tried, automatically, at a hundred others.

None of this makes you careless. It makes you normal, living in a world where your password can be involved in a crime without you doing anything wrong at all.

So the sensible question is not "how do I make my password impossible to steal?" It is: "If my password is stolen tonight, what stops the thief at the door?"

The answer is the second lock.

What the second lock is

The official name is multi-factor authentication — a forbidding phrase that means something friendly: proving who you are in two different ways instead of one. You will also see it called two-step verification, or shortened to 2FA. They all mean the same thing.

Think of your front door. A password is the key in the lock. The second step is the chain on the inside — or, better, a guard who asks to see something only you would have. The first proof is something you know: your password. The second is something you have: your phone, or a small physical key. A thief who steals the key still does not have the phone in your pocket.

This is not a hobbyist's trick. It is standard official advice: the FBI's guidance to families on protecting accounts includes turning on multi-factor authentication, and it is the step officials return to again and again, because it works even when the password has already failed.

When it is switched on, signing in looks like this: you enter your password as usual, and then the account asks for the second proof before it opens. That pause — ten seconds of mild inconvenience — is where most account theft quietly dies.

The four kinds of second lock, honestly ranked

Not all second locks are equal, and you deserve the honest order rather than the sales pitch.

1. Codes by text message. The account sends a short code to your phone by text, and you type it in. This is the most common kind, and the easiest to start with, because nearly everyone already has the equipment: a phone that receives texts. It is a genuine lock, and far better than a password alone — Margaret's morning proves it. But it is the weakest of the four. A text can be redirected if a criminal manages to take over your phone number (a trick the FBI lists among the methods of organised online crime groups), and a code can be tricked out of you by a confident caller — more on that in a moment, because it matters.

2. An authenticator app. This is a small, free app on your phone that makes a fresh code every thirty seconds, just for you. The code never travels by text and is not tied to your phone number, so the number-takeover trick fails against it. The codes and the accounts live together in the app, each with its name beside it. Slightly more setup than texts; a noticeably stronger lock.

3. A security key. A small physical object — it looks like a modest memory stick — that you plug in or tap when you sign in. There is no code to read out, repeat, or be tricked into sharing, because the key itself does the proving, silently, with the real website and no other. Of the traditional second steps, this is the strongest. Its downsides are practical: it costs a little money, and it can be lost, which is why careful people register two and keep the spare somewhere safe.

4. A passkey. The newest kind, and where things are heading. A passkey replaces the password-and-code dance altogether: instead of typing secrets, you unlock the account the way you unlock your phone — with your face, your fingerprint, or your phone's own PIN. The proof never leaves your device, so there is nothing to leak in a breach and nothing to type into a fake page. Many large services now offer passkeys alongside the older methods. Where one is offered for an important account, it is worth taking.

The honest summary: any second lock beats none, and the strongest one is the one you will actually keep switched on. Do not let the perfect be the enemy of the protected.

The one warning that matters

Everything above can be undone by a single sentence spoken aloud. So here is the warning, plainly:

A login code is a key. Never read one to a caller, and never type one into a page a caller sent you.

Remember Chapter 1 — the call that isn't your bank. The caller's whole craft is urgency and authority: there is fraud on your account, we are stopping it together, I just need the code we sent you to confirm it is you. Understand what is really happening in that moment. The caller has your password already — bought, leaked, or tricked out of someone. They have typed it in. The account has done exactly what you fitted the second lock to make it do: it has refused to open without the code. The code is on your phone. So the thief's last remaining move is to persuade you to hand it over — dressed up as the bank helping you.

Do not. A real bank never asks for the codes texted to your phone — not to "stop a payment," not to "verify your identity," not ever. The same is true of every genuine company: if a human being is asking you for a login code, that human being is the crime in progress.

This is not theory. In cases prosecuted by the U.S. Department of Justice, criminals have taken over hundreds of people's social media accounts using exactly this lever — persuading victims, many of them teenagers, to hand over account recovery codes, then walking in with them. A code feels harmless to share; it is only six numbers. But in the wrong hands it is not information. It is entry.

The rule that keeps you safe is short enough to teach the whole family: codes only ever go into the app or website you opened yourself — never to a person.

Turning it on: email, banking, social — in that order

You do not need to do every account today. Do the three that matter most, in this order, because each one guards the others.

First, your email. As the last chapter explained, email is the reset button for your digital life: forgotten-password links for nearly everything arrive there. Protect the inbox and you protect the road into everything else. Open your email app or website, find the settings — look for the words "Security" or "Sign-in" — and switch on two-step verification. Choose the authenticator app or a passkey if offered; texts if those feel like too much today.

Second, your money. Banking, cards, payment apps, investments. Most banks now offer a second step, often as an approval tap inside their own app. Turn it on, and turn on sign-in alerts too, so a stranger's attempt announces itself the way Margaret's did.

Third, your social accounts. These are the ones used to impersonate you to your family — the "I need help" message sent from a taken-over account is a standard opening move in the scams from Part I. Give the accounts your family actually gathers on the second lock as well.

Three practical notes while you are in the settings:

  • Save the recovery codes. When you switch a second step on, most services offer a set of one-time backup codes, in case you lose your phone. Print them or write them down and keep them somewhere genuinely safe — a locked drawer, not a note stuck to the screen. They are spare keys; treat them like spare keys.
  • Tell the service your new number when you change it. If your codes arrive by text, an old number can lock you out. Update it the same week you change phones.
  • Start with one account, tonight. Ten unhurried minutes on your email is worth more than a grand plan for the weekend that never happens.

Margaret's story ended with tea because a stranger, holding her correct password, met a locked second door and left. That is all this chapter asks: make the thief's best day end the same way.

If this happened

Do these now, in this order. Small steps, nothing technical.

1. Never use that code — and never read it out. If a sign-in code arrives that you did not ask for, do not type it into any page, and never read it aloud to a caller or send it to anyone who asks. A code only ever goes into the app or website you opened yourself.

2. Go to the account yourself, and change the password. Open the official app, or type the address you know — never a link from a message. Change the password to a fresh, long one, used nowhere else.

3. Turn on the second lock. In the same settings, switch on two-step verification if it was not already on — a passkey or authenticator app if offered, texted codes if not.

4. Check the account’s recent activity. Look for sign-ins or devices you do not recognise, and sign out every session that is not yours.

5. If money is involved, call your bank. If the account touches money, or someone did get in, call the bank on the number on the back of your card — never a number from a message.

6. Report it. Internet crime can be reported to the FBI’s IC3 at ic3.gov, and fraud to the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Keep the messages and the times — calm records help.

Let a stolen password be a dead end, not a doorway.
